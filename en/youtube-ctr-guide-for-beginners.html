CTR & the algorithm

YouTube CTR Explained: A Beginner's Guide

Impressions click-through rate is one of the most watched — and most misread — numbers in YouTube Studio. This guide explains exactly what it measures, why a "good" CTR depends on context, and what to do when yours looks low.

What is impressions click-through rate?

Impressions click-through rate (CTR) is the percentage of times a viewer saw your thumbnail on YouTube and then clicked to watch. YouTube calculates it as:

CTR = views from impressions ÷ impressions × 100

So if your thumbnail was shown 10,000 times and 450 of those impressions led to a view, your CTR is 4.5%.

What counts as an impression?

YouTube counts an impression when a thumbnail is shown to a viewer on YouTube — for example on the home page, in search results, in the "Up next" suggestions or in the Subscriptions feed — under specific visibility conditions (YouTube's documentation describes a thumbnail being on screen for more than a second with at least half of it visible). Some places are not included, such as views that come from external websites and end screens. That means a video's total views can be higher than its "views from impressions".

What is a good CTR on YouTube?

YouTube's own Help Center gives the most useful benchmark: half of all channels and videos have an impressions CTR between 2% and 10%. It also notes that brand-new videos and channels, or those with fewer than 100 views, can see an even wider range.

Beyond that, there is no universal "good" number, because CTR depends heavily on who is seeing the thumbnail:

Traffic sourceTypical CTR patternWhy
YouTube searchOften higherViewers are actively looking for that topic.
Subscriptions / notificationsOften higherViewers already know and like the channel.
Suggested videosVariesDepends on how closely your video matches the one being watched.
Home / browse featuresOften lowerViewers are casually browsing; YouTube shows your video to many people who weren't looking for it.

This is why the best comparison is always your own videos, for the same traffic source. A 3% CTR from the home feed can be a great result, while 3% from search on a very specific query may signal a packaging problem.

A falling CTR isn't always bad newsWhen a video starts doing well, YouTube shows it to wider audiences who are less familiar with you. CTR usually drops as impressions grow — but total views keep rising. Always read CTR next to impressions and views, never alone.

Where to find your CTR

  1. Open YouTube Studio and go to Analytics (for the whole channel) or open a single video and select Analytics.
  2. Look for Impressions and Impressions click-through rate, usually shown in the reach or content section along with how viewers find the video.
  3. For more detail, open Advanced mode and break CTR down by traffic source. This is the view that tells you the most.

Why CTR matters (and why it isn't everything)

CTR tells you how well your packaging — thumbnail, title and topic — turns impressions into views. If it's low, you're getting fewer views than you could from the exposure YouTube is already giving you.

But YouTube has been clear that it recommends videos based on viewer satisfaction, not clicks alone. It weighs signals such as watch time, survey responses, likes, dislikes and shares. A video with a high CTR that people abandon quickly won't be recommended for long. Our article on CTR vs. watch time explains how the two work together.

The three things that drive clicks

  1. The idea. Is the topic something your audience wants right now? A brilliant thumbnail can't rescue a topic nobody is curious about.
  2. The thumbnail. Is it readable at small sizes, with one clear focal point? See our complete thumbnail guide.
  3. The title. Does it make a specific, honest promise that complements the image? See our guide to titles that get clicks.

Your first five steps to improve CTR

  1. Find your baseline. Note the median CTR of your last 10–20 videos for each main traffic source.
  2. Spot the outliers. Which videos are well above or below that baseline? Compare their thumbnails and titles side by side.
  3. Fix packaging on low-CTR videos that still get impressions. Older videos that appear in search are especially worth improving.
  4. Test instead of guessing. Use Test & Compare to try two or three genuinely different options.
  5. Check new thumbnails before publishing with the thumbnail checklist and the feed preview.

Common CTR myths

  • "Anything under 4% is bad." Not true on its own. It depends on traffic source, niche and how widely YouTube is showing the video.
  • "A high CTR means clickbait." Not necessarily. Specific, well-packaged videos shown to the right audience naturally earn high CTRs.
  • "YouTube stops showing videos with low CTR." CTR is one of many signals. YouTube looks at how viewers respond overall, including whether they watch and enjoy the video.
  • "Changing the thumbnail resets the video." There's no evidence of this. You can update a thumbnail at any time.

Frequently asked questions

Why is my CTR high but my views are low?

Your video is probably getting few impressions. The packaging works for the people who see it, but YouTube hasn't shown it widely yet — often because the topic has limited demand or the video is new.

Why is my CTR low but my views are high?

That's usually a sign of success: YouTube is showing the video to large, broad audiences on the home feed, where CTR is naturally lower.

Does CTR include Shorts?

Shorts are mostly watched in the swipeable Shorts feed, where viewers don't choose from thumbnails, so impressions CTR is much less meaningful for them than for long-form videos.
