Thumbnail design

Typography for YouTube Thumbnails

Thumbnail text has to be read in a fraction of a second at a tiny size, often on a phone. That changes the rules: fewer words, heavier fonts, stronger contrast. Here's how to make text work — and when to leave it out.

Text is the most over-used element in thumbnails. It's tempting to explain the video in the image, but the title already does that. Good thumbnail text is closer to a sticker than a sentence: a few words that add emphasis, context or a twist. Below is a practical approach to deciding what to write and how to make it readable.

First, decide whether you need text at all

Ask what the words would add that the image and title don't. Text earns its place when it:

  • Adds context the image can't show — a number, a timeframe, a price, a comparison ("$0 vs $500").
  • Adds tension or a point of view — "Don't buy this", "It worked?", "Finally".
  • Labels something unfamiliar — the name of a product or place most viewers wouldn't recognize from the picture.

If the text would simply repeat the title, drop it and let the image breathe. Many of the clearest thumbnails in any niche have no text at all.

How many words?

As a working rule, keep thumbnail text to one to four words. Every extra word makes each letter smaller and slows reading. If you can't say it in four words, the idea probably belongs in the title — see our guide to writing titles for how to split the message between the two.

Choosing a font

At small sizes, readability comes from thick strokes, open letter shapes and generous height. That generally means bold or black sans-serif typefaces. Thin, script, decorative and heavily stylized fonts break down first.

Condensed vs wide

Condensed fonts let you make letters taller in the same width, which is useful for single words. Wide fonts feel friendlier and are easier to read in mixed case. Pick one and use it consistently so your thumbnails become recognizable.

Size: bigger than you think

A practical target is text whose letters are at least about 15% of the thumbnail's height, and often more for a single word. The real test is visual: shrink the thumbnail to around 168 pixels wide (the size of the desktop "Up next" column) and check that every word is still readable. The feed preview tool shows this instantly.

Contrast: make text separate from the image

Thumbnail text almost always sits on top of a photo, so it needs help to stand out:

  • Stroke (outline): a thick dark outline around light text is the most reliable technique on busy backgrounds.
  • Drop shadow: a soft, dark shadow adds separation without looking heavy.
  • Solid block: placing text on a colored box or banner guarantees contrast and can become part of your brand.
  • Color: white and bright yellow are the most legible text colors on dark backgrounds; black works best on bright blocks. Our color theory guide explains why brightness contrast matters more than hue.

Placement

  • Keep clear of the bottom-right corner, where the duration badge sits.
  • Don't cover faces, especially eyes and mouth — they carry the emotion.
  • Use the empty space created by the composition: the side opposite the subject, or the top of the frame.
  • Stack short lines rather than using one long line. Two lines of two words are easier to read than one line of four.

Hierarchy and emphasis

If you have more than one word, decide which one matters most and make it bigger or give it the accent color. For example, in "ONLY $5?", the price is the point, so it gets the size and color. Avoid using three different colors or fonts in the same thumbnail; one font and one accent color is usually enough.

ALL CAPS or mixed case?

All caps looks bold and works well for one or two words. For three or four words, mixed case is often easier to read because word shapes are more distinct. Either is fine as long as it's consistent and large.

Numbers and symbols

Digits ("7", "$500", "30 days") are compact and read faster than spelled-out numbers, which makes them useful in thumbnails. Symbols like "?", "vs" or arrows can replace whole phrases. Just make sure the number in the thumbnail is accurate and matches the video.

Text checklist

  1. Does the text add something the title doesn't?
  2. Is it four words or fewer?
  3. Is it in a heavy sans-serif font, with an outline, shadow or block behind it?
  4. Is it readable at 168 pixels wide?
  5. Is it away from the bottom-right corner and off the subject's face?

Frequently asked questions

What font do big YouTubers use?

There's no single font. Many use heavy, condensed sans-serifs or custom-drawn lettering. What they have in common is thick strokes, large size and strong contrast, which you can get with free fonts like Anton, Bebas Neue or Montserrat Black.

Does thumbnail text help SEO?

YouTube hasn't said that text inside thumbnail images is used for ranking. Put your keywords in the title and description. Thumbnail text is for people, not search.

Should I translate thumbnail text for other languages?

If a large part of your audience speaks another language, text-free thumbnails travel better. Otherwise, keep the language of the thumbnail consistent with the title.
