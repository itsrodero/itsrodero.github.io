How to Write YouTube Titles That Get Clicks — Without Clickbait

A good title tells the right viewer exactly why this video is worth their time — in a few words, before it gets cut off. Here are the principles and formulas that work, what research says about negative framing, and where the line with clickbait is.

Titles do three jobs at once. They help viewers decide whether to click, they tell YouTube's search and recommendation systems what the video is about, and they set the expectation that your video then has to meet. Most weak titles fail at the first job because they're vague: "My Thoughts on Budgeting" tells the viewer almost nothing about what they'll get.

Principles before formulas

  • Be specific. Specific details (numbers, names, timeframes, outcomes) make a promise concrete. "How I Cut My Grocery Bill to $250 a Month" beats "How to Save Money on Groceries".
  • Lead with the important words. Titles get truncated in many places. Put the topic and the hook in the first few words.
  • Write for a person, not an algorithm. Use the words your audience would use, naturally. Keyword stuffing reads badly and doesn't help.
  • Complement the thumbnail. The title and thumbnail are read together; they should add to each other rather than repeat. See our thumbnail guide.
  • Keep the promise. The video must deliver what the title says — ideally early.

How long should a YouTube title be?

YouTube allows up to 100 characters, but how much is visible depends on the device and surface: search results and the home feed can show more, while the "Up next" column and many mobile layouts cut titles off after roughly two lines. A practical rule:

  • Make sure the first ~50 characters make sense on their own.
  • Use the rest for helpful detail (a year, a qualifier, a secondary keyword), not for the core idea.

Curiosity: open a question, then answer it

Curiosity is one of the strongest reasons people click. A title creates it by pointing at something the viewer wants to know but doesn't yet: an unexpected result, an unexplained fact, the answer to a comparison. The difference between curiosity and clickbait is simple: curiosity is paid off in the video; clickbait isn't. "The Cheapest Espresso Machine That Actually Works" is curious and honest if the video names it and shows it working.

What research says about negative framing

Negative framing ("mistakes", "stop doing", "the problem with") attracts attention for a well-documented reason: people tend to weigh potential losses and threats more heavily than equivalent gains, a pattern psychologists call negativity bias.

A large study published in Nature Human Behaviour in 2023 analyzed tens of thousands of headline A/B tests run by the news site Upworthy. It found that, for a headline of average length, each additional negative word increased click-through rate by about 2.3% (a relative increase), while positive words slightly reduced it.

That's useful — but notice what it doesn't say. The effect per word is modest, the study is about news headlines rather than YouTube specifically, and more clicks don't mean more satisfied viewers. Use negative framing when it's genuinely the most useful angle (warnings, common mistakes, things to avoid), and avoid manufacturing fear or outrage. Viewers who feel manipulated leave early, and YouTube's recommendations take that into account.

Good use of negative framing"7 Budgeting Mistakes That Keep You Broke" — useful if the video explains real mistakes and how to fix them.
Poor use: "Your Bank Is Stealing From You (Watch Before It's Too Late)" — alarmist, vague, and likely to disappoint.

Where clickbait crosses the line

YouTube's spam and deceptive practices policy prohibits titles and thumbnails that trick viewers into clicking a video that doesn't deliver what was promised. Beyond the rules, these patterns tend to backfire:

  • Promising something the video never shows ("You won't believe what happened" with nothing surprising).
  • Exaggerated claims that the content can't support ("This one trick doubled my income").
  • Implying someone famous is involved when they aren't.

Titles and search

For videos people will look for, the title should contain the words they search. A few practical tips:

  • Type your topic into YouTube's search bar and look at the autocomplete suggestions — they reflect real searches.
  • Use the main phrase once, naturally, near the start. Don't repeat keywords.
  • Support the title with a clear description and, for longer videos, chapters.
  • If you have a large audience in another language, YouTube lets you add translated titles and descriptions in YouTube Studio, which are shown to viewers in that language.

Style: caps, emojis and punctuation

Capitalizing one key word can add emphasis; writing the whole title in capitals reads as shouting and is harder to scan. Emojis can work in some niches but take up space and rarely add meaning. Question marks and colons are useful for structure. Whatever you choose, be consistent across your channel.

Test your titles

YouTube's Test & Compare lets you test up to three titles (or title-and-thumbnail combinations) on the same video. Because it picks the winner by watch time, it naturally favors titles that attract viewers who stay.

Title checklist

  1. Does the title make one specific promise?
  2. Do the first ~50 characters work on their own?
  3. Does it include the words your audience would search for?
  4. Does it add to the thumbnail rather than repeat it?
  5. Will the video deliver on it — early?

Frequently asked questions

Should I include the year in my title?

Only for content that genuinely changes over time (rules, prices, rankings), and update it when the information changes. Adding a year to evergreen content doesn't help and can make it look outdated later.

Can I change a title after publishing?

Yes. Updating a title is normal, especially for older videos that no longer match how people search. For a fair comparison between options, use Test & Compare.

Are question titles better than statements?

Neither is better in general. Questions work well for explainers; statements work well for results and how-tos. Test both on your own audience.
